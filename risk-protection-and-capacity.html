Risk, Protection and Capacity

How systems change our relationship with risk

Research & Ideas · Risk · Protection · Capacity

The starting point was not really “risk management”. I had been thinking about change and the question was “what have I been seeing?”, especially things I do not understand or can’t quite wrap my head around. Then, “is there a pattern?” and if there is, “does that pattern have a bigger effect?”

I noticed that I kept coming back to the same kind of problem: What happens when individuals interact with systems designed to manage something for them?

That is where risk became a useful way of looking at the problem.

The thought that started it

The Poplar tower-block fire brought the thought closer to home. It made me think about the Grenfell fire, but not because the circumstances were identical. It was something more basic; we live surrounded by systems designed to protect us from risks we know exist.

Fire regulations. Building standards. Financial safeguards. Insurance. Compliance systems. Risk models. Warning systems. Procedures. Controls.

We create these things because individuals cannot reasonably manage every danger themselves. And that is a good reason to have them. But then, what happens to our relationship with a risk when something is constantly standing between us and it?

The risk has not necessarily disappeared. A building can still burn. A financial institution can still fail. Fraud can still happen. A crisis can still occur. But perhaps there is a difference between knowing something is possible and genuinely feeling its possibility.

I started thinking about what I call the “risk meter” or “danger meter.” The risk is known. The individual may even understand what the risk means, but how close does it actually feel?

There is a difference between: “I know this is dangerous”, “I genuinely believe this could happen to me” and “I know what I would do if it happened.” Those are not the same things and they can all exist at once. Then it led to another question: can protection change the felt proximity of risk without changing the existence of that risk?

What happens when protection becomes very effective?

The purpose of protection is to reduce exposure to harm but if it becomes very effective, what else changes? If people rarely experience the consequences of a particular risk, how do they develop a relationship with that risk?

People do not need to experience disasters in order to learn. In fact, people should not be exposed to danger simply so that they can develop capacity. But if the system successfully prevents the event, where does meaningful engagement with the risk come from? Perhaps through simulation, exercises, near misses, case studies, scenario planning or learning from other people's failures. But is information about a risk the same thing as being prepared for it?

A warning can exist. A procedure can exist. A risk assessment can exist. An organisation can know about a risk, but does that mean the people within it will know what to do when the situation changes for the worse?

The training-wheels problem

A bicycle analogy helped me think about this.

Training wheels are there to prevent someone from falling while learning to ride. That protection is useful because it helps them eventually develop the capacity to balance.

If we transpose this idea, are there systems where the protection never quite gives way to capacity? Not because the system is badly designed. Not because protection itself is undesirable. But because the system becomes the thing people rely on, rather than the thing helping them develop their own judgement.

So, when does protection support capacity, and when does it begin to substitute for it? This distinction matters.

Does protection change behaviour?

James Hedlund offered a different perspective on this. His discussion on safety regulation and risk compensation asks whether measures designed to make people safer can influence behaviour in ways that offset some of the safety gains.

The point is not that safety measures necessarily make people reckless. The more interesting possibility is that people respond to changes in perceived risk.

If the environment becomes safer, behaviour can change.

Hedlund also discusses Sam Peltzman's work which treated risk as something people can respond to when making choices. If a safety measure reduces one kind of risk, behaviour may adjust in response to the change in the environment, but this questions how we measure those effects.

Hedlund also discusses estimates of fatalities prevented by safety measures. But what exactly does a number such as “fatalities prevented” mean? A death that was prevented cannot be observed. It is a counterfactual.

So, prevented compared with what? What assumptions establish the starting point? What behaviour would have occurred without the intervention? How confidently can an outcome that never happened be measured? The number may still be useful but the number alone does not tell us the assumptions that produced it. This led to a series of questions.

If protection can influence behaviour, could it also influence what we notice, what we prepare for, or what we believe requires our attention?

If someone else absorbs some of the downside of a risk, does my relationship with that risk change?

Interestingly, this is what economists call moral hazard.

What if someone else bears the downside?

Pernell and Jung's work on government protection and bank risk-taking brought another perspective to the questions. The logic is direct: if an actor does not bear the full consequences of a risk, their behaviour might change. The possibility that shifting who bears the downside of risk can alter behaviour is intuitively powerful enough that economists have spent decades debating it but the literature complicates it.

Their work on government protection and bank risk-taking did not support the simple expectation that greater protection necessarily produces greater risk-taking. That matters because it means that the question should not be “does protection make people take more risks?” The evidence is not that simple. The better question should be “if someone else absorbs some of the downside, what happens to that person's relationship with the risk?” That is a different question and it does not assume the answer.

What happens when the system starts doing the watching?

Modern systems do a great deal of watching for us. They monitor transactions, calculate risks, issue alerts, identify anomalies and amongst other things, determine whether something has crossed a threshold. That is necessary as no individual can watch everything. But then what happens when the system becomes better at watching than the individual?

A compliance system can tell an analyst what looks unusual. A model can turn uncertainty into a number. A procedure can tell someone what to do but knowing what the system identifies is not necessarily the same as understanding the underlying risk.

This produced an important thought: the person learns to read the system, not the situation.

That does not necessarily mean they become less capable. They may become extremely good at operating the system but what happens when something falls outside the system’s indicators? What happens when the risk changes shape? What happens when the situation does not look like the thing the system was designed to identify?

If a system tells us what to look for, do we become better at recognising risk or simply better at recognising what the system has defined as risk?

A system can resolve an event without producing learning

The UK Government's work on organisational learning created a different line of thought; that an organisation can possess knowledge without necessarily developing capability from it.

A risk analysis can exist. A procedure can exist. A lesson can be documented. People can be informed, but something still has to happen between information and capability. So, this suggests a relationship:

Experience → interpretation → learning → capacity.

This means experience on its own is not enough. An experience does not automatically mean the organisation has learned from it. Therefore, a system can resolve an event without learning from it. An immediate problem can be solved. An alarm can be dealt with. An incident can be contained. A procedure can work but what did the individual or organisation actually learn? And did that learning extend beyond the particular form the event took?

This implies that simply providing people with more information does not create preparedness and information is not preparedness.

But do people actually need all that capacity?

It would be simplistic to say that systems make people less capable. People cannot maintain expert knowledge about every risk they might encounter. They don't need to.

For example, an air-traffic controller does not need to know how to fly every aircraft they direct. A compliance analyst does not need to understand every technical detail of every transaction they monitor. A resident does not need to understand the engineering behind every fire-safety feature in their building. So, the question is not “should everyone understand everything?” It is “What knowledge needs to remain with the individual because of the risks they are exposed to or responsible for?”

That makes capacity relative. Capacity should correspond to exposure, responsibility, role and frequency of interaction with the risk. A person does not need capacity for every possible danger but perhaps they need enough capacity to recognise when something does not fit. Enough to question an output, to recognise an anomaly and to notice when circumstances have moved beyond what the system was designed to handle.

What exactly is “capacity”?

Systems themselves are a form of collective human capacity. They contain accumulated knowledge. They allow expertise to be distributed. They allow people to do things they could not do individually. So how much of the knowledge contained within a system is actually usable by the people operating within it? A system may hold far more knowledge than the people within it can recognise, interpret or apply when circumstances change.

An organisation, for example, can possess enormous amounts of knowledge. That does not mean everyone who needs it can recognise it, interpret it or act on it when circumstances change. People who “know” hand down lessons to people who are supposed to absorb them. So, once again, does knowledge being available mean that capacity exists?

Meaningful engagement rather than experience

Whether people need to experience risk in order to develop capacity is another question. But that feels too simple.

Experience can teach the wrong lesson. Surviving a car crash does not mean seatbelts are useless1. An organisation surviving a crisis does not necessarily mean its preparation was adequate. So, experience itself cannot be the answer. What matters is meaningful engagement. That could involve direct experience, simulations, drills, scenarios, near misses, case studies, learning from other organisations' failures or testing assumptions. The important part is not simply encountering the event. It is understanding why it happened and what it means.

What if the system itself is the capacity?

Perhaps the system is not replacing human capacity. Perhaps it is human capacity.

Regulation contains accumulated knowledge. Technology contains accumulated knowledge. Risk systems contain accumulated knowledge. Institutions preserve knowledge across generations of people. So where does the individual end and the system begin?

If a system contains the capacity that individuals no longer need to possess themselves is that a problem, or is that what progress looks like?

It opens a set of questions about institutionalised knowledge, technology, financial systems, business development and how knowledge is preserved when it is no longer held directly by every person.

What happens when the system hasn't actually failed?

We tend to think of system failure as something breaking but what if nothing breaks? What if the system works exactly as designed and the problem is that the world has changed?

A model can calculate correctly while its assumptions become less representative2. A regulation can be followed while the underlying risk evolves. A procedure can be executed perfectly while circumstances move beyond the situation it was designed around. So, the system hasn't necessarily failed. Reality has moved beyond it and that gives us a different concept of resilience. The problem is no longer simply whether the system is strong enough. It is whether there is enough capacity around the system to recognise when the system no longer fits the environment.

The 2008 question

The financial crisis expanded my thoughts on this.

Financial institutions had increasingly sophisticated ways of measuring risk. Yet the crisis exposed weaknesses in how risk was being measured and managed3.

The Bank for International Settlements discussed how market-sensitive risk-management practices could contribute to procyclical behaviour — where responses to the economic environment can end up reinforcing the direction in which the environment is already moving. In periods of low volatility, for example, risk models could show lower measured risk, giving institutions more room within their risk limits to take on additional exposure.

The question I found more useful than simply asking whether the models were “wrong” was “what happens when the thing designed to help us see risk also changes what we see as risky?” The measure is no longer simply observing the environment because it has become part of the environment. That is a broader question than Value at Risk34. It is about the relationship between measurement and behaviour.

If a system tells us what risk looks like, people may begin responding to the measurement itself. So, can the system change the thing it is measuring? If a system changes behaviour, then it changes the environment in which the risk exists.

If a risk model changes the way institutions behave, the resulting behaviour can affect the risk that the model is supposed to measure.

If a compliance system defines what gets escalated, people learn what gets escalated.

If regulation defines acceptable behaviour, organisations adapt around the regulation.

Where then, does the system end and the environment begin?

Where does the system end and human risk perception begin?

Perhaps there isn't a clean boundary.

Human → System → Human

One way I eventually started thinking about the relationship was:

Human → System → Human

Humans identify a problem.

Humans build a system around it.

The system becomes effective.

People adapt to the system.

Then the environment changes.

Someone has to recognise that the old way of seeing the problem no longer fits. That person may be a specialist. It may be a regulator, an employee or an ordinary person interacting with the system. The important point is not that everyone needs to become an expert. It is that the system cannot be the only thing capable of recognising its own limits.

Protection and preparedness are not necessarily the same thing

This may be the distinction underneath much of the thinking.

Protection asks “how do we reduce the likelihood or consequences of the risk?”

Preparedness asks “what happens if the protection is not enough?”

Those objectives can coexist. A strong system can reduce harm enormously, but it does not automatically tell us what capacity remains outside the system. The existence of a safety net does not necessarily tell us whether anyone underneath it knows how to stand.

Simply put, protection should be a guide, not a pillar. Not because pillars are inherently bad. But because if the entire structure depends on the pillar never failing, what happens when it does?

The question I keep returning to

The thinking began with protection. Then behaviour. Then perception. Then attention. Then learning. Then capacity. Then the limits of systems and each question seemed to create another rather than settle the previous one. And I kept thinking, what happens if the protection never gives way to capacity?

I don't necessarily think there is an answer. It may simply be the point where the questions have become clearer. Perhaps the bigger question is:

If a system becomes very good at protecting people from a risk, what happens to the human capacity to recognise, understand and respond to that risk when the system is no longer enough?
