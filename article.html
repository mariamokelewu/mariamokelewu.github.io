Regulation, Business Growth and the Room to Thrive

How regulatory and economic policy choices affect smaller and growing businesses

Research & Ideas · Regulation · Business · Economics

Businesses do not encounter public authorities only when they pay taxes or breach certain rules. They encounter them in the conditions that govern market access and operation, hiring, transacting, raising capital, reporting, expanding and growing.

I started thinking about this through something quite simple: two businesses can be subject to the same rule and experience it very differently. For a large organisation, a new requirement may be absorbed into systems, processes and teams that already exist. For a smaller business, the same requirement may land with an owner who is also managing sales, employees, cash flow and day-to-day operations.

The rule is the same. The effect does not have to be.

So when rules are designed, how much consideration is given to the different capacity businesses have to absorb their effects?

This becomes more complicated when we move beyond the idea of the small business as a single category. Mallett, Wapshott and Vorley argue that there is no single “small business effect” of regulation because businesses differ in their circumstances and in how they respond to regulatory requirements.

That made me think about what happens between the creation of a rule and its eventual effect on a business.

Regulation is not experienced in the abstract

Regulation is often discussed as though the important thing is the rule itself: what it requires, who it applies to and what happens when it is breached.

Businesses experience something more complicated.

A business first has to work out which rules apply to it. It then has to understand what they mean for the way it operates, decide what to do and adjust accordingly. Mallett et al. describe this as a process of identification and interpretation, strategisation, negotiation and adaptation.

This is where the effect of a rule becomes harder to measure. A £10,000 expenditure can be counted. The value of an owner's time, a delayed investment, a postponed expansion or a decision not to enter a market is much harder to see. And that matters because the cost of a rule is not necessarily the same thing as its economic effect.

The same rule does not necessarily produce the same effect

The difference is not necessarily the rule itself, but the capacity of the business to absorb its effects.

This is where the distinction between equal treatment and equal effect becomes more puzzling.

A rule can apply equally to two businesses while producing very different consequences. That does not mean a smaller business should automatically receive an exemption. A small business can still create significant risks, and size alone does not tell us everything about a firm's financial position, business model or ability to absorb a particular requirement.

So what should?

Revenue? Number of employees? Profitability? Cash reserves? The complexity of the business model? The risks created by the business? The systems and resources it already has?

Van Stel, Storey and Thurik's research is useful here because it shows that different forms of regulation do not necessarily have the same relationship with entrepreneurship. Their study found that minimum capital requirements and labour-market regulations were associated with lower entrepreneurship rates, while administrative requirements around starting a business were not significantly associated with the formation of nascent or young businesses.

That makes the relationship more complicated than simply saying that regulation prevents businesses from starting.

It also raises a bigger question: which part of the business journey are we actually talking about?

Starting a business is one thing. Surviving is another. Expanding is another again.

What happens when regulation meets the real economy?

The commercial world is rarely as neat as a regulation might appear on paper.

Consider businesses involved in the trading of fuel and other regulated products. Their ability to operate can depend on approvals, record-keeping, transaction requirements, fraud prevention, reporting and administrative processes.

The commercial objective is straightforward: procure the product, manage costs and make a profit.

The regulatory environment adds another layer around that activity, as businesses may need to demonstrate that transactions are legitimate, maintain records, provide information to public authorities and navigate administrative arrangements connected with rebates or other requirements.

None of these requirements is necessarily unreasonable on its own. Preventing fraud and protecting public revenue are legitimate objectives.

The question is what happens when the individual requirements begin to accumulate.

At what point does the regulatory environment become significant enough to affect how the business allocates its time, money and attention?

And if that happens, is that effect being captured when the regulation is assessed?

Regulatory fit

There is another problem.

Businesses do not stay still.

Markets change. Technology changes. Consumer behaviour changes. Business models change. Entrepreneurs find new ways of solving old problems, and sometimes create entirely new markets in the process.

Regulation has to respond to these changes, but it does not always move at the same speed.

A rule may have made sense when it was introduced but become unnecessarily difficult as the market changes. Equally, a new business model may create risks that existing rules were never designed to address.

This is where regulatory fit becomes important.

The question is not simply whether a rule exists, but whether it still fits the environment in which businesses are operating.

Mallett et al.'s review is useful here because it shows that regulatory effects can depend on the wider regulatory environment, including its stability and consistency. Businesses can adapt to rules, but constant changes to those rules can create another kind of difficulty: uncertainty about what they will have to adapt to next.

Does regulation leave enough room to grow?

This is where regulation starts to become an economic question.

Growth requires more than having a good idea. A business needs to manage cash, hire people, invest in technology, respond to customers and take calculated risks.

A reporting requirement may consume managerial time. A licensing requirement may delay market entry. A new requirement may require technology that a larger company can introduce relatively easily but a smaller business has to fund from limited resources.

This does not mean that those requirements should simply disappear.

It means that how rules are designed shapes the environment in which businesses make commercial decisions.

And perhaps this is where regulatory policy becomes economic policy.

The 2024 research on the quality and quantity of entrepreneurship clearly reveals this distinction. It separates the amount of entrepreneurial activity in an economy from the type of entrepreneurship taking place, including innovation-oriented activity. It suggests that different regulatory costs and procedures can affect these outcomes in different ways.

So perhaps the question is not simply whether regulation creates more or fewer businesses.

It is also what kinds of businesses, ideas and activities the environment encourages.

A business may respond to regulation by changing its systems, changing its business model, avoiding a particular market or finding another way to deliver its product.

Some of these changes may be useful adaptations.

Others may simply reflect where the business has had to direct its limited resources.

What does it actually mean to thrive?

This is where the discussion becomes more difficult.

We often talk about business growth as though it can be captured by a number: more employees, higher turnover, greater output.

But growth is not necessarily the same thing as thriving.

The UK Government's review of the evidence on regulation and SME growth points out that firm-level evidence is surprisingly limited. It also highlights an important problem with measuring growth itself: businesses can grow in turnover without increasing employees, or increase their workforce without necessarily improving their financial performance.

The research does not give us a simple answer to whether regulation helps or hinders SME growth.

If we only ask how much a rule costs, we may miss what happens afterwards. If we only count how many businesses are created, we may miss what happens to the businesses that already exist. And if we only measure growth through employees or turnover, we may miss the decisions businesses make before they ever reach that point.

We also have to consider what it means for a business to actually thrive.

Is it simply surviving within the rules?

Or does it mean having enough room to build, invest, adapt, innovate and pursue opportunities?

A question of design

The UK already has processes for assessing the effects of proposed regulation on smaller businesses. The Better Regulation Framework and Small and Micro Business Assessment are intended to consider the costs and impacts of regulatory proposals and whether those impacts can be mitigated.

That leads to another question: if the potential costs of regulation are already being assessed, how much of the eventual experience of the business can those assessments actually capture?

A calculation can estimate the direct cost of a new requirement. It is much harder to predict whether an owner will delay an investment, whether a business will decide not to enter a market, whether a new employee will not be hired, or whether an entrepreneur will simply choose a different business model.

These are not necessarily failures of regulation. They may be perfectly rational business decisions.

But they are still economic effects.

And perhaps this is the part of the discussion that deserves more attention: what happens after the policy leaves the page and reaches the business?

Conclusion

I started with a fairly simple question: what happens when the same rule meets businesses with very different capacities, resources and ways of operating?

The more I looked at it, the less convincing the simple answers became.

Regulation can create costs. It can also protect consumers, prevent fraud, establish standards and support the conditions in which markets function. Some forms of regulation appear to affect entrepreneurship more than others. Some may affect whether businesses enter a market, while others may influence how they grow or what kind of activity they pursue.

The effect is therefore not necessarily found in the rule alone.

It is found in the interaction between the rule and the business.

Mallett et al.'s work captures this well. Businesses do not simply receive regulation and absorb its cost. They interpret it, strategise around it, negotiate it and adapt to it. The economic consequences can therefore be indirect, difficult to measure and different from one business to another.

Perhaps the more useful question is not whether there is too much or too little regulation.

It is whether the rules governing businesses leave enough room for them to adapt, invest, grow and pursue opportunities.

That, to me, is what creating “room to thrive” really means.
